GenCast walkers · FourCastNet 3 score · 21-day lead · N = 64 · nine CONUS events

AI+RES Insights

What rare-event resampling can say about a week-3 forecast that a contingency table cannot: a probability for events the whole ensemble misses, the depth at which it stops working, and the two places where the answer can be checked against direct sampling.

Every number on this page reduces to one observable:

AL = cos(lat)-weighted mean, over the event box, of the 7-day-mean 2 m temperature anomaly ending at the event peak

GenCast 0.25° is initialised 21 days before that peak and rolled as 64 walkers. At each of five barriers FourCastNet 3 scores every walker's expected AL; high scorers are cloned, the rest are killed, and a weight wi corrects for the resulting tilt, so the surviving population still estimates GenCast's own probabilities. Each walker carries a probability mass pi = Z·wi/N, the headline P(AL ≥ obs) is the sum of pi over the walkers past the observation, and ∑pi over the whole population is the run's own normalization check.

∑pi should be 1 and is not. It runs 0.568 to 1.769 across the nine productions and reaches 8.75 on the persistence control. That is why no probability appears anywhere on this page without it.

What carries the uncertainty here, and what does not. A founder-lineage bootstrap over the walker population was considered and deliberately left unbuilt: the weights are not exchangeable, the estimator under resampling is not obvious, and this experiment has a documented case of failing silently and plausibly. So exactly three already-measured numbers do the work, and no new statistic is invented:

1. the direct-sampling comparison with its 95% Wilson interval, wherever direct sampling resolves at all; 2. the weight ESS out of 64, which is 2.24 on Winter Storm Uri and 1.01 on the control; 3. the run's own normalization check ∑pi. Every AI+RES probability on this page is a point value with no interval, on purpose.

Two notations in that table. A weighted P written as 0 (< 3.2e-04) is a run where nothing reached the observation: the estimate is exactly 0 and the bracketed number is the probability mass of the single walker that came closest, i.e. the finest the run could have resolved had one walker crossed. 0.5827 pinned is a run where every walker reached, so P collapses onto ∑pi and the two columns print the same number; section C takes that case apart.

Section B

The result

On six of the nine events every member of the direct ensemble misses the observation: 0 of 24, or 0 of 16 on South-Central. A contingency table sees TP = 0, FP = 0, TPR = 0 on all six and cannot tell California's 0.01 K miss from Elliott's 4.60 K one. AI+RES returns a number in every case, and the number tracks how deep the observation sat in the model's own distribution.

Panel 2The severity ladder

source figures/aires/aires_insights_ladder.png · from runs/aires/<event>/ds_baseline.json, runs/aires/<event>/res/<tag>/compare.json

This is the source deck's magnitude axis done properly. Fixed ±2/3/4 °C thresholds are thresholds on the observation; the quantity that actually predicts whether resampling reaches an event is a property of the forecast distribution:

σ = tail_sign × (observed − mean(direct)) / sd(direct, ddof = 1)

using the first available of the GenCast xres cube, the Gate 3 walkers and FCN3. Derived from each run's own compare.json and ds_baseline.json, and it reproduces the published ladder. ddof = 1 is not cosmetic: the population sd moves the Pacific Northwest from +1.98 to +2.02 and the Southwest from +3.32 to +3.39.

Winter Storm Elliott at −14.42 K is reached. The Southwest heat wave at +5.14 K is not, and in absolute terms the Southwest is the shallower event. The difference is that Elliott sits +2.81 σ into GenCast's own week-3 spread and the Southwest sits +3.32 σ. The reach boundary lies between +2.85 σ (California, reached) and +3.32 σ (Southwest, not reached).

Read the two ends of this figure carefully. The Southwest point is an upper bound, not an estimate, and the p90 2023-11-07 point is not a probability at all. Neither is a small number that happens to be uncertain; each is a different kind of statement, and the marker shape is what distinguishes them.

Panel 3Every walker, its anomaly, the mass it carries

source figures/aires/aires_walkers.png · from runs/aires/aires_walkers.csv

The raw material every other figure on this page reduces. Each panel carries a line at 1/N, which is what a walker would be worth under direct sampling: draw N members independently and each one carries exactly 1/N and no more. AI+RES buys reach by giving that uniformity up, and then charges for it. The Pacific Northwest's hottest walker carries 2.5% of what a single direct member is worth. Reach is bought and then priced, and this panel shows the exchange rate per walker.

Panel 4The population as a density

source figures/aires/aires_kde.png · from runs/aires/aires_walkers.csv

The same 64 walkers smoothed into a probability density over AL. Three curves per run: weighted by pi, which is the estimator's own reconstruction of GenCast's forecast law; unweighted, which is where resampling actually pushed the population and is not a probability; and the direct ensemble, which estimates the same law as the weighted curve and is therefore the like-for-like comparison.

Every curve stops at its own most extreme member. A Gaussian kernel is never zero, so an unclipped curve puts mass past an observation that nothing reached, and on the persistence control it would show 0.035 of probability beyond a target that 0 of 24 direct members attained. The panel titles print the exact subset sum P, never a kernel integral: unclipped smoothing overstates P by 1.15× to 1.75× depending on the run.

Section C

Is it calibrated

The reliability question, answered at the precision the data actually supports: two anchors deep in the tail where direct sampling still returns a non-zero count, one median check that passes, and one below-median case where the estimator degenerates and returns a plausible-looking number that is wrong.

Panel 6The two calibration anchors

source figures/aires/aires_insights_anchors.png · from runs/aires/p90_20251224/res/pilot/compare.json, runs/aires/WinterStorm_Elliott_2022/res/pilot/compare.json, aires/aceiling.py::wilson

At the depth AI+RES operates, direct sampling reads 0 of 24 on six of the nine events, so on those six the weighted curve has nothing at all to be checked against at the observation. Elliott's CONUS number is a second anchor, not Elliott's score. The event is scored on its N Plains box, where the observation is −14.42 K at +2.81 σ and direct sampling reads 0/24. On the CONUS mean the same week verifies at −2.73 K, which is +1.89 σ and where direct reads 1/24. Both are real; they are different quantities and the deck never substitutes one for the other.

The reason this matters beyond one label: on the CONUS index four of the nine events sit at or below the median, the Pacific Northwest among them at −0.93 σ: the 2021 heat dome is +7.72 K over its own box and +0.64 K over CONUS, and 19 of 24 direct members are already past that. That is precisely the regime the next panel shows the estimator has no resolution in, and it is why the box index is the observable everywhere else on this page.

Both intervals span a factor of 27, because 1 of 24 is the shallowest non-zero a 24-member ensemble can report. That width is the result's precision, and it is the reason the cheapest available upgrade to this experiment is extending the direct-sampling reference rather than running new events: GenCast member seeds are fold_in(PRNGKey(0), i), so the existing 24-member cube extends by rolling members 24 onward, at roughly 24 H100-hours per event.

Panel 7The control that passes and the case that fails, at the same size

source figures/aires/p90_20240802/aires_walkers_p90_20240802_pilot.png, figures/aires/p90_20231107/aires_walkers_p90_20231107_pilot.png, figures/aires/p90_20240802/aires_pdf_conus_p90_20240802_pilot.png · from runs/aires/p90_20240802/res/pilot/compare.json, runs/aires/p90_20231107/res/pilot/compare.json

Two more comparisons resolve against direct sampling, but in the middle of the distribution rather than in the tail, so they are checks rather than anchors. One is the method working where it should and one is the method failing where it must, and putting them on one page at the same size is the deck's credibility.

This is the deck's most useful negative result and it is reported at full size. AI+RES has no resolution at a target the tilt has already carried the entire population past. The usable operating range is targets the resampled population still straddles: p90 2024-08-02 straddles its target (population minimum +0.39 K against a target of +1.06 K) and answers correctly; p90 2023-11-07 does not and returns a pinned value.

Two things make it worth publishing rather than burying. First, the failure is silent: nothing inside the run reveals it, and a reader of compare.json alone would take 0.5827 at face value. Self-normalising is no better, returning exactly 1.000, because "every walker reached it" is the whole of what the population retains. Second, run health does not detect it: this run has the healthiest sampler of the nine, with per-step ESS/N of 1.00 / 0.41 / 0.62 / 0.53 / 0.53 and a minimum of 0.41, the highest minimum on the slate. A healthy sampler can still return a meaningless probability if the target is on the wrong side of the tilt.

The spatial evidence on the median check is the cleanest agreement anywhere in the experiment. At the derived per-grid-point threshold of +3.952 K, the p95 of the observed field, the weighted population gives 0.0727 against direct GenCast's 0.0702, agreement to 4%, while the unweighted population gives 0.1627, which is 2.3× too heavy. The weights do their job on the spatial distribution and not only on the scalar. Do not quote the 63/64 reach count as a headline here: 63 of 64 walkers reaching a median target is a statement about where the tilt pushed the population and carries no information.

CFSv2 reaches the observation on 0 of 4 members on all six genuinely extreme events, and on the warm-spell anchor as well, seven of the nine in all. The only two it reaches are the two mildest, at 3 of 4 and 2 of 4, on the +0.21 K and +1.06 K CONUS-mean days. That pattern, the operational baseline being competitive exactly where the event is not extreme, is the cleanest single statement of what this method is for. "Reaching" is sign-aware, so on the two cold events it means far enough below.

Two caveats belong with that table, both measured rather than argued. Resolution is not the explanation. Round-tripping each event's ERA5 truth field through CFS's own T126 grid and re-scoring it costs a mean of +0.010 K and a maximum of 0.134 K, and a native-cell estimator that does no regridding at all differs by at most 0.167 K, against gaps of 2.3 to 11.6 K on the six genuinely extreme events. AL is a 7-day mean over a box that is 36 deg² at its smallest, 132 deg² on Uri and the whole of CONUS on the three p90 cases, and coarsening a field that is about to be averaged over an area that size barely moves the average. Resolution would matter for a point maximum, a spatial percentile or a precipitation extreme; it does not matter for this index. The two mild rungs carry an error bar comparable to their own gap. p90 2024-08-02's gap is +0.01 K, the same order as the discretisation term, so "CFS was exactly right there" is not a precise result.

The spread between four cycles six hours apart is itself worth noticing: the Pacific Northwest runs −2.03 to +3.42 K and Elliott −10.74 to +3.93 K. That is the lagged ensemble's own spread at S2S lead. Do not read a single cycle as "what CFS said".

Section E

Space, and how far this can be pushed

A box mean can be attained by a field with the wrong structure, and a 21-day rollout can stop being an atmosphere without saying so. Both are measured here.

Section F

What this does not say

This page is a page, not a footnote. The nine events answer one question well and several adjacent questions not at all, and the difference is structural rather than a matter of sample size that more compute would fix.

Panel 14The limits

source no figure · numbers from runs/aires/<event>/res/<tag>/compare.json, runs/aires/aires_walkers.csv, runs/xres/0p25/week3/cache/HurricaneIan_2022_cube.nc

The unit of analysis is the event, and there are nine of them. A hindcast verification climatology's unit is the (date, gridcell) sample and it has between 2.3×105 and 7.0×105 of them; skill is a contingency table summed over that population and every stratification is a partition of it. Nine events are not a partition of anything. Worse, the 64 walkers of one event are not 64 samples of that kind: they are one importance-weighted estimate of one tail probability, and their weight ESS is 5.68 for the Pacific Northwest, 2.24 for Uri, 7.24 for the warm anchor and 1.01 for the control. The honest count is nine independent probability estimates, several resting on the equivalent of two to seven independent pieces of information.

  • No ROC curve and no AUC. Nine events give a nine-point ROC whose AUC has a standard error near 0.2, which cannot distinguish 0.5 from 0.9. Reaching a precision where differences of 0.01 to 0.05 are meaningful needs of order 103 events, which at ~64 H100-hours each is of order 6×104 H100-hours. That is a design mismatch, not a funding gap: AI+RES is expensive precisely because it does something a bulk-skill metric does not need.
  • No per-gridcell skill map of any kind, for the same reason from the other side: a per-cell frequency needs many dates per cell, and this experiment has one initialisation per event. A per-cell count over nine events is 0, 1/9 or 2/9.
  • No by-year, by-season or per-market panel. The nine events span 2020 to 2025 and three seasons, with zero in spring. Five events per season is twenty events, roughly 1,280 H100-hours, and would still leave binomial intervals spanning about 0.2 to 0.8. The boxes are event-centered windows, 6° × 6° on five of the six named events, rather than service territories, and AL is a box mean, so re-boxing is a full re-run of every event.
  • No calibration in the bulk of the distribution, and one outright wrong answer. This is a measured property of the estimator and not a sample-size limit: on p90 2023-11-07, at −0.97 σ, it returned 0.5827 against a true 0.875 from direct sampling's 21/24, and 0.5827 is exactly that run's own normalization check. It has no resolution at a target the tilt has already carried the whole population past, it gives no sign of it from inside the run, and the run's health metrics were the best on the slate. A verification deck's central region, where the base rate is around a quarter, is exactly where this method is worst.
  • No claim of unbiasedness. The design says so explicitly, because a large same-model direct-sampling reference was descoped. What the experiment supports is that resampling efficiently reaches the tail of GenCast's forecast distribution and recovers observed extremes a 24-member ensemble misses. Every probability here is unbiased for P as GenCast defines it; if the model's own conditional tail is wrong, every stream inherits that and no amount of reweighting reveals it.
  • Nothing about precipitation or wind at this lead. Hurricanes were evaluated and rejected: on its registered 850 hPa wind-speed metric, Hurricane Ian puts the observation at −0.60 σ, below the ensemble mean, with 17 of 24 direct members already exceeding it. A 7-day-mean box-mean wind washes out a one-day landfall. That is a property of the reduction, not a tuning problem.
  • The nine events are not a sample of anything. They were selected because they happened and because they were extreme. Treating them as a draw would be selection-biased in exactly the direction that flatters the method.
  • No interval on any AI+RES probability. A founder-lineage bootstrap is the obvious next statistic and it was deliberately not built: only 11 to 17 of 64 founders survive per run, the weights are not exchangeable, and whether Z is recomputed per draw and how the tied mass blocks are handled are both open. Inventing that here is the single highest-risk act available, and section C is the standing proof that this experiment can fail silently and plausibly.

Two extensions are specified and unbuilt, both requiring GPU time. A lead-time sweep of 3 events × 4 leads at days 14, 18, 24 and 27 costs about 768 H100-hours and would turn panel 13 from a stability statement into a skill statement; it is inside the walker's measured stable range. Extending the direct-sampling reference from 24 to 96 members costs about 24 H100-hours per event, roughly 216 for all nine, and is the cheapest single upgrade available: it widens the range over which the weighted curve can be checked at all, which is what panels 5 and 6 rest on.

Every figure and every number on this page comes from files already on disk under runs/aires/ and runs/astab/. The three new figures are drawn by aires/ainsights.py; the rest were written by aires/aplots.py, aires/awalkers.py, aires/akde.py, aires/amaps.py, aires/apdfs.py and astab/plots.py and are collected, not regenerated. scripts/embed_insights.py inlines each one as a WebP data URI, because the Artifact content policy blocks every external host.

The σ-depths are derived from each run's own compare.json and ds_baseline.json rather than quoted, the walker masses are aires.alift.walker_mass imported rather than reimplemented, the only interval anywhere is aires.aceiling.wilson applied to a direct-sampling count, and the stability verdict comes from astab.reduce. Contract tests for all four are in aires/tests/test_ainsights.py.

Cost of what is shown: about 232 H100-hours for wave 1 (five runs plus one gate) and about 318 for wave 2 (five productions plus one gate), at roughly 64 H100-hours and 8 hours of wall time on one 8×H100 node per production.
