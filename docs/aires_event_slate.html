AI+RES Event Slate

Nine CONUS cases run through rare-event resampling at week-3 lead, ranging from a heat dome that no direct ensemble reaches to a day sitting at the ensemble median. Each case is reported with its observed anomaly, its percentile against 64 years of ERA5, and the probability the run assigns to it.

Every number on this page reduces to one observable:

AL = cos(lat)-weighted mean, over the event box, of the 7-day-mean 2 m temperature anomaly ending at the event peak

GenCast is initialised 21 days before that peak and rolled as 64 walkers. At each of five barriers FourCastNet 3 scores every walker's expected AL. Walkers with high scores are cloned, the rest are killed, and a weight wi corrects for the resulting tilt, so the surviving population still estimates GenCast's own probabilities. The C schedule (0, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8, 2.0) was tuned once, on the Pacific Northwest case, and has not been re-tuned since.

The severity ladder

Cases are ordered by how far the observation fell outside GenCast's own week-3 forecast distribution. That distance, rather than the size of the anomaly or its climatological rarity, predicts whether resampling reaches the observation. Winter Storm Elliott at −14.42 K is reached. The Southwest heat wave at +5.14 K is not, because it lies 0.5 σ deeper in the model's spread.

The two percentile columns answer different questions. The climatological percentile measures how rare the weather was, from the same index computed over 64 years of ERA5 and pooled within ±21 days of the event's calendar position. The σ-depth measures how hard the event was to forecast, from where the observation fell in the 24-member GenCast ensemble launched three weeks earlier. The South-Central heat dome is the rarest case in the table and only the fifth hardest to reach.

The whole population

all ten runs cross-event

Every number in the severity-ladder table is a sum over the walkers of one run. The population figure below gives that raw material, one panel per run, in the same hardest-first order. Each panel shows where the 64 surviving walkers landed on AL and the probability mass pi = Z·wi/N that each carries. The shaded band is the mass that sums to the headline P.

Deeper reach costs probability mass. The blue line is 1/N, the mass a walker would carry had it not been resampled, and the deepest walkers lie one to two decades below it. The hottest Pacific Northwest walker carries 2.5 % of the mass of a single direct member.

The masses come in tied blocks. Walkers cloned at the last barrier inherit their parent's mass exactly, so the 64 walkers of Winter Storm Uri hold 34 distinct values and those of the persistence control hold 15. That count, not 64, is the number of independent pieces of information behind the estimate.

Two runs cannot answer their own question. The Southwest heat wave and the persistence control have no walker in the band, so P is 0 and the statement the run supports is the deepest level it resolved, 3.2 and 2.6 × 10−4 respectively. The 7 November 2023 case fails in the opposite way. Its observation lies below every walker, so the band covers the whole panel and P equals the run's own normalization check.

The trajectory panel in each case section also carries the operational CFSv2 forecast at the same 21-day lead, for all nine events. CFSv2 reaches the observation on the two mildest rungs only, with 3 of 4 members on 7 November 2023 and 2 of 4 on 2 August 2024, and misses every extreme case. It misses the 24 December 2025 warm spell by 4.6 K, with its ensemble mean on the opposite side of climatology from the observation. That miss is not a resolution artefact. Regridding the ERA5 truth field onto the CFS ~0.94° grid and re-scoring it moves AL by at most 0.13 K, against gaps of 2.3 to 11.6 K.

Uri did its damage through duration and through minimum temperature in a region without winterisation, and AL measures neither.

The cause is structural and was checked numerically. Resampling tilted the whole population warm, so its lowest realized AL is +1.498 K, above the +0.211 K target, and the indicator 1{AL ≥ observed} equals 1 for all 64 walkers. The estimate Z·mean(w·1{...}) then reduces to Z·mean(w), which is the normalization check, 0.582662 against 0.582662 to every digit printed. The estimator cannot return a probability above its own normalization check, and the observed value lies above that ceiling. Self-normalizing does not help, since it returns 1.000, the only information the population retains being that every walker reached the target. Nothing inside the run reports this condition, and a reader of compare.json alone would take 0.583 at face value.

AI+RES therefore has no resolution at a target that the tilt has already carried the entire population past. The usable operating range is the set of targets that the resampled population still straddles. The Normal-Day Test case meets that condition, with a population minimum of +0.39 K against a +1.06 K target. This case does not.

The persistence control

same walkers, same schedule, different score attribution not reached

This run attributes the gains reported above to the FCN3 score. It repeats the Pacific Northwest pilot with the same walkers, the same resampling, the same C schedule and the same seeds, and changes only the function that decides which walkers to clone, from a forecast of the event to the current-state temperature. It reaches the observed +7.72 K with 0 of 64 walkers.

The selection carries no information at this lead. ESS/N falls to 0.04 and 0.02 at steps 3 and 4, with clone multiplicities of 39 and 49, three of 64 founders survive, and the weights span 0.013 to 869. The normalization check comes out at 8.75 with a weighted mean of +0.29 K, so the weights cancel the tilt. Cloning on a forecast of the event outperforms cloning on the present state, as the Gate 3 persistence column predicted before these runs were made.
