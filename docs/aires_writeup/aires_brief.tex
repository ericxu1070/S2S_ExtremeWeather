\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Three weeks before an extreme weather event, a conventional forecast ensemble of 24 members does
not produce it. The event is rarer than 1 in 24, so the ensemble returns no member at the
observed value and the forecast carries no information about the tail. We report results from
AI+RES, a rare-event sampling method that runs 64 forecast trajectories and resamples them five
times over the forecast, discarding the trajectories that do not approach the event and
duplicating those that do. The resampling is driven by a second, independently trained AI weather
model, which forecasts each trajectory's outcome at the target week and ranks the population by
it. On the seven test cases at or beyond the 96th percentile of the 1959 to 2022 climatological
distribution, AI+RES produced forecast members that reached the observed anomaly in six cases.
Direct ensemble sampling reached it in none of the seven, and the operational Climate Forecast
System version 2 (CFSv2) in none of the seven. One AI+RES forecast takes 7.2 to 8.7 hours and 61
to 64 H100 GPU-hours on a single eight-GPU node, about \$155 at \$2.50 per GPU-hour. Obtaining
the same set of extreme trajectories by direct sampling is estimated at 5 to 23 times that cost,
and the multiple grows with the rarity of the event. Nine test cases support a qualitative
assessment. They are not enough to establish a hit rate, a skill score, or a calibrated
probability, and no such claim is made here. Every number below is measured from a run unless it
is marked as an estimate.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Weather forecasts are run as ensembles. The same model is launched many times from slightly
different starting points, and the spread of the outcomes is the forecast. The number of runs
sets the smallest probability the ensemble can resolve. With 24 runs, nothing rarer than about
1 in 24 can be distinguished from zero, and a single run landing on the event is
indistinguishable from chance.

Three weeks out, the events that matter most are the ones in that blind spot. The 2021 Pacific
Northwest heat dome ran 7.72\,K above normal over its region for a week, which for that region
and season is about a 1-in-22-year week (Table~\ref{tab:results}). A 24-member GenCast ensemble
launched three weeks earlier produced no run that reached it. CFSv2 at the same lead forecast a
week near normal. Neither model is wrong about the atmosphere. Both are being asked a question
their sample size cannot answer.

\section{The method}
\label{sec:method}

Rare-event sampling concentrates a fixed compute budget on the trajectories that approach the
event, instead of spreading it evenly over the forecast distribution. We launch 64 forecast
trajectories from the same starting conditions. Every three days we stop them, score each one,
discard the low scorers, and duplicate the high scorers in their place. The population stays at
64 members throughout and is progressively concentrated on the trajectories heading toward the
extreme. Weights carried alongside each trajectory convert the concentrated population back into
an unbiased probability at the end.

The score is the step that decides the outcome. The conventional choice is the trajectory's
current temperature, which three weeks before a heat wave carries no information about which
trajectory becomes hot. We instead score each trajectory by running a second, independently
trained AI weather model forward from it and taking its forecast for the target week. At the
9-day checkpoint of the 2021 heat dome, the AI score ranks the trajectories with a rank
correlation of 0.80 against where they in fact ended up. The conventional score reaches 0.06 at
the same checkpoint. Run on the identical setup with only the score exchanged, the conventional
method reaches the observed 2021 heat dome with 0 of 64 trajectories.

The trajectory model is GenCast (Google DeepMind) at 0.25-degree resolution. The scoring model is
FourCastNet~3 (NVIDIA). Both were trained on data ending before 2019, so every event tested here
is out of sample.

\textbf{Lead times are not identical across the columns of Table~\ref{tab:results}.} AI+RES
forecasts from a 21-day lead. CFSv2 is also at 21 days, as four operational cycles launched 6
hours apart. Direct sampling is a 24-member GenCast ensemble at a 14-day lead in every row except
the S.\ Texas case, where it is 16 GenCast runs at 21 days. A separate 24-member FourCastNet~3
ensemble at 21 days was run on five of the nine cases and reached the observed value on one of
them, the 2021 heat dome, with one member. The direct-sampling column therefore had a one-week
shorter lead than AI+RES in most rows, which favours it, and it still reached none of the seven
extremes.

\textbf{Rarity is what separates the methods.} Where the event is rare, AI+RES produces members
that reach it and the two alternatives do not. Where the event is ordinary, which is the case for
the two controls in Table~\ref{tab:results}, all three methods reach it. The controls are the
check that the difference between methods is a function of rarity and not of one model being more
accurate in general. The single miss, the 2020 Southwest heat wave, is the rarest case in the set
by return period. Resampling moved the population 1.75\,K toward the observed value and stopped
4.4\,K short of it.

\textbf{The bound on what this configuration reaches is qualitative.} On the evidence of these
nine cases, the configuration tested here, 64 trajectories with five resampling steps at a 21-day
lead, produces the event for extremes up to about the 99.9th percentile, which is roughly a
1-in-40-year week for a given region and season. It reached one event with no analogue in the
64-year record, the S.\ Texas heat dome, and missed another, the Southwest heat wave at an
estimated 1-in-210-year return period. The boundary for this budget is therefore near the
1-in-100-year level rather than beyond it. Past that boundary the limit is population size, which
scales linearly in compute. Nine cases do not establish a hit rate, and the percentile above
should be read as a working expectation rather than as a measured threshold.

The saving in Table~\ref{tab:savings} is not the primary result. At the \$156 budget of
Table~\ref{tab:cost}, direct sampling returns no members at all on these events, which is the
measured 0 of 7 in Table~\ref{tab:results}. The cost multiple grows with rarity, from 5x on the
least rare event priced here to 23x on the rarest, because direct-sampling cost scales as the
reciprocal of the event probability while AI+RES cost is flat in it. The 2020 Southwest heat wave
is absent from Table~\ref{tab:savings} because neither method reached it. Direct sampling would
need an estimated 3,300 runs, about \$3,300, for a single member, and AI+RES at this budget did
not reach it either.

\section{What the result supports}
\label{sec:scope}

The result supports a specific claim. On nine out-of-sample cases, concentrating a fixed compute
budget on the trajectories heading toward an extreme produces forecast members on events that
direct sampling misses entirely at the same or a larger budget, at a measured \$153 to \$160 and
7.2 to 8.7 hours per forecast.

It does not support treating these numbers as a calibrated probability service. Three limits
qualify the result.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Nine events do not establish a hit rate.} The 6-of-7 figure of
  Table~\ref{tab:results} carries a wide interval. A 42-case hindcast campaign designed to
  produce a reliability curve is built and staged, and has not been run.
\item \textbf{The probabilities carry about a factor of two.} They are internally consistent, in
  that where direct sampling can resolve a probability our estimate agrees with it within
  sampling error, but the absolute level at 64 trajectories is noisy. The method also returns no
  usable probability for an event milder than the population it has already concentrated, which
  is a property of the algorithm rather than a failure of a particular run.
\item \textbf{The sampled distribution is the forecast model's, not the atmosphere's.} The
  supported claim is that the method reaches the extreme tail of a current forecast model
  efficiently. Whether that model's tail is unbiased against the real climate is a separate
  question, and this work does not settle it.
\end{enumerate}

Three lines of work follow. The 42-case calibration campaign, at an estimated \$1,700 of GPU time
for its first 12-case tier, would replace the qualitative bound of Section~\ref{sec:results} with
a reliability curve. A scoring function for tropical cyclones is needed because the weekly
regional average used here averages away a one-day landfall. Lighter model pairs would reduce the
per-forecast cost directly, since the current pair requires an 80\,GB GPU per trajectory.
