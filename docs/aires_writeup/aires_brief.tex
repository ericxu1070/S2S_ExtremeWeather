\date{\vspace{-2.2em}}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A conventional forecast ensemble does not produce extreme events. An ensemble of $N$ members
cannot resolve an event whose probability is below about one in $N$: on average no member lands at
the observed value, and the forecast carries no information about the tail. We report results from AI+RES, a rare-event sampling method that
runs 64 forecast trajectories and resamples them five times over the forecast. At each
resampling step it scores every trajectory by its forecast outcome and prunes the population toward
the extreme. The score comes from a second, more efficient AI weather model, which forecasts each
trajectory's outcome from the resampling step to the target lead time; the population is ranked by
that forecast outcome. On the seven test cases at or beyond the 96th percentile of the 1959 to 2022 climatological
distribution, AI+RES produced forecast members that reached the observed anomaly in six of the seven.
Direct ensemble sampling reached it in none of the seven, and the operational Climate Forecast
System version 2 (CFSv2) in none of the seven. One AI+RES forecast takes 7.2 to 8.7 hours and 61
to 64 H100 GPU-hours on a single eight-GPU node, about \$156 at \$2.50 per GPU-hour. Obtaining
the same set of extreme trajectories by direct sampling is estimated at 5 to 23 times that cost, and the multiple grows with the rarity of the event. Nine test cases support a qualitative assessment while we
currently work on a more quantitative result.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}
\label{sec:problem}

Weather forecasts are run as ensembles. The same model is launched many times from slightly
different starting points, and the spread of the outcomes is the forecast. The number of runs
sets the smallest probability the ensemble can resolve. An ensemble of $N$ runs resolves
probabilities down to about one in $N$ and no further: an event rarer than that is, on average,
absent from every run, and if one run does land on it, that single run cannot be told apart from
chance. The direct ensembles in this brief have 24 members, so their floor is about 1 in 24, a 4\%
probability.

At a lead of three weeks, the time from launch to the event, the events that matter most are the
ones below that floor. The 2021 Pacific
Northwest heat dome ran 7.72\,K above normal over its region for a week, which for that region
and season is about a 1-in-22-year week (Table~\ref{tab:results}). A 24-member ensemble of GenCast, the
AI weather model of Section~\ref{sec:method}, launched three weeks earlier produced no run that
reached it. The operational Climate Forecast System version 2
(CFSv2), launched at the same time, forecast a week near normal. Neither model is wrong about the mean state of the atmosphere. Both are limited by the same
two things: too few runs to resolve an event this rare, and an ensemble spread that may itself
under-represent extremes, so that more runs alone would not be certain to fix it.

\section{The method}
\label{sec:method}

The algorithm is AI+RES, introduced by \citet{lancelin2026airres}, whose schematic of the
method is reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:lancelin}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{figs/lancelin_fig1.png}
\caption{The AI+RES algorithm, reproduced from Figure~1 of \citet{lancelin2026airres}.
\textbf{(a)} A population of trajectories runs forward in the physics model.
At each resampling time, marked by a dashed vertical, the low scorers are discarded (red crosses)
and the high scorers duplicated in their place (green crosses). \textbf{(b)} The score comes from
an ensemble forecast made by the AI model from each trajectory's current state out to the target
time, which is what carries information the trajectory's present state does not. \textbf{(c)} The
run yields a catalogue of extreme events and \textbf{(d)} an unbiased return-period curve, at a
fraction of the cost of direct sampling (DS in the panel). The configuration reported here follows the same
schematic with GenCast as the trajectory model and FourCastNet~3 as the scorer.}
\label{fig:lancelin}
\end{figure}

Rare-event sampling concentrates a fixed compute budget on the trajectories that approach the
event, instead of spreading it evenly over the forecast distribution. We launch 64 forecast
trajectories from the same starting conditions. Every three days we stop the trajectories, score each one,
discard the low scorers, and duplicate the high scorers in their place. The population stays at
64 members throughout and is progressively concentrated on the trajectories heading toward the
extreme. Weights carried alongside each trajectory convert the concentrated population back into
an unbiased probability at the end.

The score is the step that decides the outcome. It is a single number assigned to each
trajectory at a resampling step, meant to predict how extreme that trajectory's target week will
turn out to be. The population is ranked by it, the lowest-ranked trajectories are discarded and
the highest-ranked duplicated, as in panel (a) of Figure~\ref{fig:lancelin}. The standard choice
of score is persistence, the trajectory's regional temperature anomaly at the moment it is scored.
Persistence does not tell you what the trajectory does by the end of the forecast. In the 2021
heat-dome run, at the 9-day resampling step, the persistence ranking of the 64 trajectories bears
almost no relation to the ranking of their final outcomes: the rank correlation between the two is
0.06, where 1 would be a perfect ordering and 0 none.

We score instead with a forecast, as in panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:lancelin}. A second, more
efficient AI weather model is run forward from each trajectory's current state to the event, and
the score is that model's 6-member ensemble mean of the regional temperature anomaly over the 7
days ending at the event peak. The window is 7 days because that is how long the heat wave itself
lasts. Ranked by this score, the same 64 trajectories at the same 9-day step have a rank
correlation of 0.80 with their final outcomes, against 0.06 for persistence. That difference
decides the result. When the 2021 heat-dome run is repeated with persistence as the score and
every other setting unchanged, none of the 64 trajectories reaches the observed anomaly; with the
forecast score, 42 of 64 do (Table~\ref{tab:savings}).

The target here is heat waves. A trajectory scores higher the hotter the week it is forecast to
produce, so the population is pulled toward heat. The direction of the score is the only part of
the method that changes with the kind of extreme. For a cold outbreak the sign is reversed and a
trajectory scores higher the colder its forecast week, which is the setting used for Winter Storms
Uri and Elliott in Table~\ref{tab:results}. For a tropical cyclone the score would be built from
low mean sea level pressure and high wind speed rather than from temperature.

The trajectory model is GenCast \citep{price2025gencast} at 0.25-degree resolution. The scoring
model is FourCastNet~3 \citep{bonev2025fcn3}. Both were trained on data ending before 2019, so every event tested here
is out of sample.

Neither choice is structural. The algorithm needs a trajectory model that can be
stopped, checkpointed and restarted, and a scoring model that forecasts to the target lead faster
than the trajectory model reaches it. Any weather model meeting those two
conditions can take either role, so a more accurate or cheaper model released later can be
substituted for one of ours. The component that would be rewritten is the adapter that translates
one model's state into the other's input format, not the sampling algorithm.

The demonstration of \citet{lancelin2026airres}, the one Figure~\ref{fig:lancelin} is drawn
from, ran an intermediate-complexity climate model as the trajectory model, scored by an AI emulator trained on that same model, and targeted heat waves in
the model's own climate. The work reported here replaces both halves with weather models
trained on the observed atmosphere, at 0.25-degree resolution, and targets named events in the
observed record.

\textbf{Rarity is what separates the methods.} Where the event is rare, AI+RES produces members
that reach it and the two alternatives do not. Where the event is ordinary, which is the case for
the two controls in Table~\ref{tab:results}, all three methods reach it. The controls are the
check that the difference between methods is a function of rarity and not of one model being more
accurate in general.

\textbf{Lead times are not identical across the columns of Table~\ref{tab:results}.} AI+RES
forecasts from a 21-day lead. CFSv2 is also at 21 days, as four operational cycles launched 6
hours apart. Direct sampling is a 24-member GenCast ensemble at a 14-day lead in every row except
the S.\ Texas case, where it is 16 GenCast runs at 21 days. A separate 24-member FourCastNet~3
ensemble at 21 days was run on five of the nine cases and reached the observed value on one of
them, the 2021 heat dome, with one member. The direct-sampling column therefore had a one-week
shorter lead than AI+RES in most rows, which favours that column, and it still reached none of the seven
extremes.

\textbf{The bound on what this configuration reaches is qualitative.} The configuration tested here
is 64 trajectories with five resampling steps at a 21-day lead. On the evidence of these nine cases
it produces the event for extremes up to about the 99.9th percentile, roughly a 1-in-100-year week
for a given region and season. It reached one event with no analogue in the
64-year record, the S.\ Texas heat dome, and missed another, the Southwest heat wave at an
estimated 1-in-210-year return period. That miss is the rarest case in the set by return period.
Resampling moved the population 1.75\,K toward the observed value and stopped 4.4\,K short of
it. The boundary for this budget is therefore near the
1-in-100-year level rather than beyond it. Past that boundary the limit is population size, which
scales linearly in compute. With nine cases, that percentile is an estimate from a small sample,
not a measured threshold. The seven extremes
were chosen as some of the most extreme events of the out-of-sample period, with no formal
selection rule.

Figure~\ref{fig:maps} shows the 2021 heat dome as maps: the AI+RES population reproduces the dome
in the observed location and at the observed intensity, and the direct ensemble average from the
same starting point contains no dome. Figure~\ref{fig:walk} follows the hottest surviving
trajectory of that run through the resampling steps.

The saving in Table~\ref{tab:savings} is not the primary result. At the \$156 budget of
Table~\ref{tab:cost}, direct sampling returns no members at all on these events, which is the
measured 0 of 7 in Table~\ref{tab:results}, most of it at the shorter 14-day lead. The cost multiple grows with rarity, from 5x on the
least rare event priced here to 23x on the rarest, because direct-sampling cost scales as the
reciprocal of the event probability while AI+RES cost is flat in it. The ordering is not by
climatological rarity: the two cold events carry the largest multiples, above the rarer S.\ Texas
heat dome, because the multiple counts the direct runs needed to match the number of members AI+RES
put on target, and AI+RES put 20 and 32 members on the cold targets against 6 on S.\ Texas.

The 2020 Southwest heat wave is absent from Table~\ref{tab:savings} because neither method
reached it. Direct sampling would need at least 3,300 runs, about \$3,300, for a single member.

\section{What the result supports}
\label{sec:scope}

On nine out-of-sample cases, concentrating a fixed compute budget on the trajectories heading
toward an extreme produces forecast members on events that direct sampling misses entirely at the
same or a larger budget, at the measured cost of Table~\ref{tab:cost}. The result does not support
treating these numbers as a calibrated probability service, for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Nine events do not establish a hit rate.} The 6-of-7 figure of
  Table~\ref{tab:results} carries a wide interval. A 42-case hindcast campaign designed to
  produce a reliability curve is built and staged, and has not been run.
\item \textbf{The probabilities carry about a factor of two.} They are internally consistent. Where
  direct sampling can resolve a probability, our estimate agrees with it within sampling error.
  The absolute level at 64 trajectories is noisy. The method also returns no
  usable probability for an event milder than the population it has already concentrated, which
  is a property of the algorithm rather than a failure of a particular run.
\item \textbf{The sampled distribution is the forecast model's, not the atmosphere's.} The
  supported claim is that the method reaches the extreme tail of a current forecast model
  efficiently. Whether that model's tail is unbiased against the real climate is a separate
  question, and this work does not settle it.
\end{enumerate}

The 42-case calibration campaign, at an estimated \$1,700 of GPU time
for its first 12-case tier, would replace the qualitative bound of Section~\ref{sec:results} with
a reliability curve. A scoring function for tropical cyclones is needed because the weekly
regional average used here averages away a one-day landfall. Lighter model pairs would reduce the
per-forecast cost directly, since the current pair requires an 80\,GB GPU per trajectory.

\section{Derivation of the cost multiple}
\label{sec:costmath}

Write $k$ for the number of AI+RES trajectories that reached the observed value, $\hat{p}$ for the
probability AI+RES assigned to the event, the weighted estimate of Section~\ref{sec:method},
$c_{\mathrm{d}}$ for the cost of one direct 21-day GenCast run, and $c_{\mathrm{a}}$ for the cost
of one AI+RES forecast. Each member of a direct ensemble lands at
or beyond the observed value with probability $\hat{p}$, so $n$ runs put $n\hat{p}$ members there
on average, and $n = k/\hat{p}$ runs are needed for that average to equal $k$. The cost of those
runs is $n\,c_{\mathrm{d}}$, and the cost relative to one AI+RES forecast is
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{n\,c_{\mathrm{d}}}{c_{\mathrm{a}}} \;=\; \frac{k\,c_{\mathrm{d}}}{\hat{p}\,c_{\mathrm{a}}},
\qquad c_{\mathrm{d}} = \$1.00, \qquad c_{\mathrm{a}} = \$156 .
\label{eq:multiple}
\end{equation}
%
Both costs are measured. $c_{\mathrm{d}}$ is 0.4 H100-hours per 21-day run at \$2.50 per GPU-hour,
and $c_{\mathrm{a}}$ is the range in Table~\ref{tab:cost} rounded to its midpoint. The 2021 Pacific Northwest
heat dome gives the smallest multiple in the set. There $k = 42$ and $\hat{p} = 0.054$, so
$n = 780$ runs at \$780, which is $780/156 = 5.0$ times the AI+RES cost. Winter Storm Uri gives
the largest. There $k = 32$ and $\hat{p} = 0.0090$, so $n = 3{,}560$ runs at \$3,560, which is
$3{,}560/156 = 22.8$ times. The five events priced in Table~\ref{tab:savings} span that 5x to 23x
range.

Only $\hat{p}$ is estimated rather than measured, which is why Table~\ref{tab:savings} is marked
as an estimate. At 64 trajectories $\hat{p}$ carries about a factor of two, and the multiples
carry the same factor. No ensemble of the sizes in the fourth column of Table~\ref{tab:savings}
was run.
