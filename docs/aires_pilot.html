Walking into the heat dome

1 · THE RUN

Every branch the pilot rolled, from the init to the peak

The observable AL is the cos(lat)-weighted mean, over a box covering Oregon and Washington, of the 7-day-mean 2 m temperature anomaly for the week ending at the event peak. The vertical axis of Fig. 1 is the same index evaluated frame by frame, that is, the value a walker holds at that moment rather than the value it will average to. It is plotted as a 24-hour running mean centred on each frame, because the raw 12-hourly index has a diurnal swing of several kelvin that on 64 lineages obscures the trend. The horizontal axis is lead time remaining to the peak, so it counts down: every lineage leaves the shared ERA5 initial condition at 21 days on the left and arrives at the peak at 0 on the right. The five dotted verticals mark the resampling barriers, which are quoted elsewhere in this page as lead from the init (3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 d) and therefore fall at 18, 15, 12, 9 and 6 days before the peak here.

What Fig. 1 does not test

Fig. 1 is not evidence that the score was right. Trajectories are drawn from the walkers’ own diagnostics and the barriers are drawn where the schedule put them, so nothing in the figure tests whether θ predicted the outcome. That test is Fig. 8 of Section 7, and it was passed before this run was submitted, as Gate 3 of Table 7.

3 · THE MATH

The score, and the rule that decides who splits and who dies

1 · The score is a forecast, not a measurement

At barrier k, walker i’s global state is converted to a 72-channel FCN3 initial condition and rolled to the event peak M times. Its score, Eq. (1), is the mean of what those M members say the observable will be.

Eq. (1) · the score θk(i)  =  (1/M)  Σm=1..M  AL( FCN3m( adapter( Xki ) ) )
Xki
walker i’s global GenCast state at barrier k, 12 prognostic variables on 13 levels, two time frames.
FCN3m
the m-th stochastic member, rolled at 6 h steps from the barrier to the peak. All M members share the same initial condition and differ only through FCN3’s own noise generator.
AL(·)
the cos(lat)-weighted PNW-box mean of the 7-day-mean anomaly for the week ending at the peak, the same function applied to the walkers themselves at the end of the run.
M = 6
so θ carries a sampling error of about 0.25 K on this event, which is enough to rank walkers and not enough to equate them.

θ is a prediction of where the walker is going, not a reading of where it is. The cheap alternative, scoring a walker by its current anomaly, is the Standard-RES of Lancelin et al., and Table 4 shows it has no skill at the leads that matter here.

2 · From score to selection pressure to offspring count

Scores in kelvin cannot drive a schedule that has to work across events with different units and spreads. The score is therefore standardized across the live population and scaled by the barrier’s selection pressure, Eq. (2).

Eq. (2) · standardized score and tilt zk(i)  =  ( θk(i) − meanj θk(j) ) / sdj θk(j)
Vk(i)  =  Ck · zk(i)

A walker’s weight is the change in V along the leg it just travelled, from its parent’s value to its own. The population mean of those weights normalizes the leg, Eq. (3).

Eq. (3) · incremental weight and its mean Wk(i)  =  exp( Vk(i) − Vk−1(parent of i) )
Rk  =  (1/N) Σi Wk(i)
Eq. (4) · expected number of children nk(i)  =  Wk(i) / Rk     Σi nk(i) = N by construction

Dividing by Rk holds the population at N. Cloning the good walkers is paid for by killing others, and that accounting is done in Eq. (4) rather than by capping or padding afterwards.

3 · From a fractional child count to an integer

nk(i) is a real number such as 4.17 or 0.21, and a walker cannot have 0.21 children. Because n is often greater than 1, which is what cloning is, this is an allocation problem rather than a selection one.

  • Guaranteed children. Every walker gets floor(nk(i)) children outright. A walker with n = 4.17 is guaranteed 4.
  • Pivotal sampling allocates the leftover fractions (Deville & Tillé, 1998). The fractions sum to a whole number, pivotal sampling hands out that many extra children, and walker i receives one with probability equal to its own fraction. It repeatedly takes two undecided walkers and moves probability mass between them until one is settled at 0 or 1, which preserves every marginal exactly and conserves the total at every move.
Two alternatives, and why they fail

Independent coin flips on each fraction give the right per-walker probabilities and a fluctuating population size, 61 walkers one step and 67 the next, which makes every downstream budget wrong. A multinomial draw fixes the total and inflates the variance of the offspring counts, which is the noise the estimator has to fight. Pivotal sampling gives exact marginals and an exact total.

A walker is killed when it gets zero children, that is, when its floor is 0 and it loses the fractional draw. Nothing else kills a walker, and there is no threshold and no ranking cutoff. The offspring vector is then flattened into a parent list, so counts [0, 2, 0, 1] become parents [1, 1, 3], and the next leg rolls one segment per child from the named parent’s state.

Rk is the mean of exp(z), and by Jensen’s inequality that mean exceeds exp(mean z) = 1. A walker sitting at the population mean score is therefore below the mean weight and at risk, with P(killed) = 0.435 in the third row of Table 1. That is why 28 of 64 walkers died at a barrier whose selection pressure was 1.0. The scheme does not preserve the middle of the population. Rk falls below 1 at later barriers, where Vk−1 is no longer zero and W measures a change rather than a level.

5 · Undoing the tilt to recover a probability

Because V depends only on state, the weights telescope along a lineage, and the product of W down any surviving branch is exp(VK − V0). After the last barrier the population is distributed under the tilted law. Any expectation under the original law is recovered by dividing the tilt back out, Eq. (5).

Eq. (5) · the estimator EP[φ]  =  Z · (1/N) Σi φ(Xi) · exp( −VK(lineage of i) )  ,   Z = Πk Rk

Setting φ = 1{AL ≥ a} in Eq. (5) gives the exceedance curve of Fig. 2. For this run Z = 1.0000 × 1.7702 × 0.5468 × 0.6787 × 0.5995 = 0.39387 and log Z = −0.9317.

Eq. (6) · population health ESSk  =  ( Σi wi )2 / Σi wi2    ESS = N under uniform weights, ESS = 1 when one walker has taken over
The Ck = 0 invariant

Set every Ck = 0. Then V ≡ 0, W = 1, Rk = 1, Z = 1, and every final weight is 1, so the algorithm reduces to direct sampling exactly rather than approximately. A unit test pins that identity, and it is the strongest end-to-end check available on the scheme. The core is also validated against an analytic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck problem whose answer is known, with no GPU involved.

Where the guarantee weakens

Eq. (5) is unbiased for a deterministic V. Standardizing the score across the live population, Eq. (2), is what lets one Ck schedule work across events with different units, and it makes V depend on the other walkers. The scheme is then self-interacting and the guarantee drops from unbiased to consistent. No closed-form confidence interval is available for the RES curve for the same reason. The band drawn in Fig. 2 treats the walkers as independent, which standardization makes them not, so it is a lower bound on the true uncertainty. The implementation accepts a fixed reference mean and standard deviation so that the strictly unbiased regime can still be tested.

5 · IN SPACE

Whether the field is the event, or only a matching box mean

AL is an area mean, and an area mean can be reached by the wrong field. A ridge displaced a few hundred kilometres, or a broad shallow warm anomaly with no dome in it, scores the same as the event. Neither Fig. 1 nor Fig. 2 separates those cases. The maps do. Every panel in Fig. 3 to Fig. 7 is the same reduction as AL on the same 105×237 grid, so walkers, direct members and observed truth are subtractable and share one colour scale.

At 21 days’ lead the baseline ensemble means in Fig. 3 have no dome in them, between +2.3 and +2.5 K against an observed +7.72 K. Their most extreme members do have one, in the right place, and land at +7.08, +7.49 and +7.87 K. The pattern is available to a 24-member ensemble, and the amplitude is reached by 1 of the 64 direct members in Table 2. Every AI+RES panel is past the observed value.

The top row of Table 3 is not a skill claim

The AI+RES population mean is the closest field to what happened, at 0.86 K, inside the 2.34 K by which ERA5 and HRRR disagree. That population was aimed at this event, so its unweighted mean is not an estimate of the forecast mean and its closeness is not evidence of better forecast skill. What it does show is what a rare-event sampler is meant to deliver, that the observed event sits inside the resampled distribution rather than at its edge. A baseline ensemble mean, which is a forecast, is 5.2 to 5.5 K away.

The tail overshoots the event. The top-10 composite and the single most extreme walker carry +2.31 and +2.58 K of box bias in Table 3. The resampling was told to maximise AL and it went past the observed value rather than stopping at it. That is the specified behaviour of the algorithm, and it means the far tail of this population is more extreme than 2021 rather than a reconstruction of it.

A single-walker case for an AI scorer

At the 9 d barrier this lineage occupied slot 63 and held the 4th-coolest current anomaly of the 64 walkers alive, −2.69 K against a population mean of +1.46 K. That is z = −1.33 on a persistence score, which is under one expected child, so a persistence score would have killed it there. FCN3 ranked it 10th highest, θ = +7.18 K, and it drew 11 children, the maximum multiplicity of that barrier. It became the most extreme walker of the run. This is one walker and therefore an illustration rather than evidence. The evidence is the aggregate in Table 4, where persistence scores ρs = 0.06 at that lead and FCN3 scores 0.80. The ridge at 9 d was already building over the Pacific while the surface under it had not yet warmed, so the score was reading the flow rather than the surface temperature.

Column 2 of Fig. 6 is the cost of resampling. The AI+RES population’s own spread over the box is 1.77 K against 2.85, 3.17 and 3.25 K for the three free ensembles. A tail-selected population of clones is tighter than a free one, which is the same finding Fig. 9 reports as a 60% separation deficit between siblings and unrelated pairs. It is why 64 resampled walkers are worth fewer than 64 independent draws.

6 · CAVEAT

Two checks on the probability, with different verdicts

Check 1 · the curves agree where both have members

Over the range +3.7 to +6.8 K, where the RES curve and the direct curves both have members, the ratio between them has a geometric mean of 1.27 and a range of 0.97 to 2.13. That is inside the sampling error of 16 to 24 independent draws, whose Wilson intervals span factors of two to three over the same range. The RES curve is not systematically displaced.

Check 2 · the total-mass check reads 0.568, not 1

An unbiased estimator assigns total probability 1 to everything. This run assigns 0.568. That is ordinary at this ensemble size rather than a defect. Replaying the schedule 2,000 times at N = 64 puts 0.568 at the 33rd percentile, and the statistic is right-skewed, with mean 0.99, median 0.73, standard deviation 1.17, and 36% of runs below 0.6. The value near 1 quoted in the estimator’s own documentation is the mean, not what a single run at this N reads.

Both checks have the same cause, and it is visible in the weights

58 of the 64 final weights are below 2 and 52 are below 1. Four walkers, weighted 6.9, 10.0, 11.2 and 34.5, carry two thirds of the total mass. Rare-event resampling abandons the bulk by design. Only 11 of 64 founders still have descendants and no walker finished below +3.54 K, so the probability mass that belongs at low AL is represented by a few heavily weighted survivors and P(everything) is noisy by construction. Below +3.54 K the RES curve in Fig. 2 is flat at 0.568 because it has no members there. That segment is the total-mass check plotted against a threshold axis, and it is not a bulk estimate.

Quote the ratios, not the absolute probabilities. Absolute probabilities from this run carry roughly a factor of two at N = 64. The ratio comparisons against direct sampling, and the reach of the population, do not.

Why an AI scorer rather than the cheap one

The Standard-RES of Lancelin et al. scores a walker by the observable’s current value, which costs nothing. At the two leads the gate was taken on, that baseline has no skill, 0.06 at 9 d and 0.13 at 12 d, both inside the noise, while FCN3 reaches 0.80 and 0.96 in Table 4. Persistence becomes useful only at 15 d, by which point the verification window has begun. FCN3 is therefore not reading off the current state, and that is what the GPU hours buy.

8 · GENEALOGY

Eleven founders, and clones that separate on their own

What the sibling separation shows

  • Sibling clones born at the 15 d barrier differ by 1.0 K RMS in 2 m temperature over CONUS after a single 12 h step, growing to about 2.5 K by the 21 d horizon. No hand-tuned perturbation was needed.
  • Unrelated walkers sit at 3.0 to 4.5 K over the same span. At the horizon siblings are separated by about 60% of the unrelated-pair distance, so clones remain correlated at verification and the effective number of independent tail samples is below 64.
  • This is the first quantitative measurement of that correlation, and it is the argument for adding the perturbation of Lancelin et al. if a later run resamples harder or later than this one did.

The stack, in one paragraph

GenCast is the walker, the object propagated, cloned and killed. FCN3 is the score function. At each barrier it is handed the walker’s state and asked, with 6 members run to the peak, where that walker is heading. The roles are inverted relative to the obvious reading of the two models, in that the diffusion model does the physics and the deterministic-core model does the judging. They cannot share a process, JAX against PyTorch, so walker and scorer hand off through disk, and the join is a 72-channel adapter in which 69 channels are a rename plus a latitude flip and 3 are derived from a fitted calibration.

12 · OPEN

What this pilot does not settle

  • One event, one atmosphere. Everything here is the 2021 PNW dome from one init. The reach claim is robust, and the probability is one draw of one schedule at N = 64.
  • Clones are not independent. A separation at 60% of the unrelated-pair distance at verification means the 64 tail samples are worth fewer than 64. Quantified, not yet fixed.
  • The box clips the event. The observed dome maximum reached about 52 N into British Columbia, outside the 50 N CONUS crop the cubes carry, so the scored box sees the southern two thirds of what happened.
  • The far tail overshoots the event. The top-10 composite carries +2.31 K of box bias and the single most extreme walker +2.58 K. That is correct for an algorithm told to maximise AL, and it means the extreme end of this population is more extreme than 2021 rather than a reconstruction of it.
  • Absolute probabilities need more walkers. The factor-of-two uncertainty on P is an N problem, and the total-mass diagnostic is the statistic to watch as N grows.
