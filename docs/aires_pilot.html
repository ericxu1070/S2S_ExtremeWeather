THE RUN

Every branch the pilot rolled, from the init to the peak

The observable is AL: the cos(lat)-weighted mean, over a box covering Oregon and Washington, of the 7-day-mean 2 m temperature anomaly for the week ending at the event peak. The y axis below is the same index evaluated frame by frame — where each walker is at that moment, not what it will average to — as a 24-hour running mean, because the raw 12-hourly index carries a real diurnal swing of several kelvin that on 64 lineages reads as noise. The five dotted verticals are the resampling barriers.

How to read it

A surviving lineage. Not one walker — a chain of segments stitched across the genealogy. Walker 3’s state at 15 d and its state at 6 d can belong to entirely different ancestries, so every trajectory here is assembled by walking the parent table backwards from the peak.
A killed branch. Its score at that barrier was low enough that the resampling gave it no children. The compute already spent on it is not wasted — it is what told the scheme where not to look — but the branch itself ends.
The population-mean score at each barrier. θ is FCN3’s 6-member forecast of the walker’s final AL, and it is the only quantity the resampling reads. It climbs +1.98 → +4.07 → +5.57 → +6.62 K across the four scored barriers — the population being steered, one barrier at a time.
The 7-day window. Lead 15 d to 21 d, thirteen 12-hourly frames ending exactly at the peak. Everything left of it is the walk; the number the whole experiment is about is a single average taken inside it. No resampling happens here — the last barrier is at 15 d, so the event window is a free 6-day run-out.
ERA5, +7.72 K. What the atmosphere did. Note where the grey branches sit relative to it: the killed lineages are overwhelmingly the ones that were heading below it.
The one thing this figure is not

It is not evidence that the score was right. Trajectories are drawn from the walkers’ own diagnostics, and the barriers are drawn where the schedule put them; nothing in the picture tests whether θ predicted the outcome. That test is the scatter panel further down, and it was passed before this run was submitted, as Gate 3.

SCHEDULE

Five barriers, each one harder than the last

At barrier k every walker is scored, the scores are standardized across the live population, and each walker is cloned a number of times proportional to exp(Ck · standardized score). Ck is the selection pressure: at 0 nothing happens, and it was ramped deliberately so the early barriers — where the score is weakest — do the least damage.

Barrier 1 is deliberately inert. At 3 d lead the score has no skill on this event — Gate 3 measured ρs = −0.04 there — so spending walkers on it would be selection on noise. The population diversity it preserves is what the later, sharper barriers get to spend.

The schedule was tuned before the run, and held

Replaying the same Ck on Gate 3’s measured skill curve predicted effective sample sizes of 1.00 / 0.43 / 0.36 / 0.38 / 0.58. The pilot delivered 1.00 / 0.28 / 0.26 / 0.46 / 0.60 against a target of N/4 = 0.25. It ran hot in the middle — barriers 2 and 3 landed near the 5th percentile of the replay — and finished essentially on the predicted value. Ck = (0, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8, 2.0) is confirmed at N = 64 on real scores.

IN SPACE

Is it the actual event, or just a number that matches?

AL is an area mean, and an area mean can be reached by the wrong field: a ridge displaced a few hundred kilometres, or a broad shallow warm anomaly with no dome in it, scores the same as the event. Neither the exceedance curve nor the trajectory plot can tell those apart. The maps can — every panel below is the same reduction as AL, on the same 105×237 grid, so walkers, direct members and observed truth are directly subtractable and share one colour scale.

At 21 days’ lead the baseline ensemble means have no dome in them at all — +2.28 to +2.54 K against an observed +7.72. Their most extreme members do have one, in the right place, and land at +7.08, +7.49 and +7.87 K: the pattern is available to a 24-member ensemble, the amplitude only barely. Every AI+RES panel is past the observed value.

Read the top row carefully — it is not a skill claim

The AI+RES population mean is the closest field to what happened, at 0.86 K, well inside the 2.34 K that ERA5 and HRRR disagree by. But that population was deliberately aimed at this event, so its unweighted mean is not an estimate of the forecast mean and its closeness is not evidence of better forecast skill. What it does say is the thing a rare-event sampler is supposed to deliver: the observed event sits inside the resampled distribution rather than at its edge. A baseline ensemble mean, which genuinely is a forecast, is 5.2–5.5 K away.

And the tail overshoots. The top-10 composite and the single most extreme walker carry +2.3 and +2.6 K of box bias — the resampling was told to maximise AL and it went past the observed value rather than stopping at it. That is correct behaviour for the algorithm and a caution for the reader: the far tail of this population is more extreme than 2021, not a reconstruction of it.

The clearest single-walker case for an AI scorer

At the 9 day barrier this lineage’s ancestor was the coolest of all 64 — −2.69 K against a population mean of −0.17 K — and it went on to be the most extreme walker in the run. A persistence score, which clones whoever is already hottest, would have killed it there. This is one walker and therefore an illustration rather than evidence; the evidence is the aggregate, where persistence scores ρs = 0.06 at that same lead and FCN3 scores 0.80. But it shows what those numbers mean: the ridge at 9 d was already building over the Pacific while the surface under it had not yet warmed, and the score was reading the flow rather than the thermometer.

Column 2 is the cost of resampling, drawn. The AI+RES population’s own spread over the box is 1.77 K against 2.85, 3.17 and 3.25 K for the three free ensembles. A tail-selected population of clones is tighter than a free one — the same finding the sibling-divergence panel reports as a 60% separation deficit, seen here as a map. It is why 64 resampled walkers are worth fewer than 64 independent draws.

CAVEAT

Is the probability trustworthy? Two checks, and they say different things

Check 1 — where both methods can be measured, they agree

Over the range +3.7 to +6.8 K, where the RES curve and the direct curves both have members, the ratio between them has a geometric mean of 1.27 and a range of 0.97–2.13. That is inside the sampling error of 16–24 independent draws, whose own Wilson intervals span factors of two to three there. The RES curve is not systematically displaced.

Check 2 — the total-mass check reads 0.568, not 1

An unbiased estimator should assign total probability 1 to everything. This run assigns 0.568. That is ordinary at this ensemble size, not a bug: replaying the schedule 2,000 times at N = 64 puts 0.568 at the 33rd percentile, and the statistic is strongly right-skewed — mean 0.99, median 0.73, sd 1.17, with 36% of runs landing below 0.6. The “should be about 1” in the estimator’s own documentation is the mean, not what a single run at this N typically reads.

Both checks have the same cause, and it is visible in the weights

58 of the 64 final weights sit near 1; the total mass is carried by about three walkers, weighted 10, 12 and 34.5. Rare-event resampling deliberately abandons the bulk — only 11 of 64 founders still have descendants, and no walker finished below +3.54 K — so the probability mass that belongs at low AL is represented by a handful of heavily weighted survivors, and P(everything) is noisy by construction. Below +3.54 K the RES curve is flat at 0.568 because it has no members there: that segment is the total-mass check drawn sideways, and must not be read as a bulk estimate.

What to quote and what not to. Absolute probabilities from this run carry roughly a factor of two at N = 64. The ratio comparisons against direct sampling, and the qualitative reach of the population, do not.

The pilot column reads lower than Gate 3, and that is range restriction, not a worse scorer. Gate 3’s 16 walkers spanned −3.2 to +7.5 K. The resampled population spans +3.5 to +10.3 K and is concentrated above +7. Correlation is attenuated by exactly that compression — and these walkers are descendants of one another rather than independent draws, so the number is not comparable to the gate’s in the first place.

Why an AI scorer, rather than the cheap one

Lancelin et al.’s Standard-RES scores a walker by the observable’s current value, which costs nothing. At the two leads the gate was taken on, that baseline has no skill at all — 0.06 at 9 d and 0.13 at 12 d, both inside the noise — while FourCastNet 3 reaches 0.80 and 0.96. Persistence only becomes useful at 15 d, by which point the verification window has already begun. Whatever FCN3 is doing, it is not reading off the current state. That is what buys the GPU hours.

GENEALOGY

Eleven founders, and clones that separate on their own

It does — and here is the honest half

  • Sibling clones born at the 15 d barrier differ by 1.0 K RMS in 2 m temperature over CONUS after a single 12 h step, growing to about 2.5 K by the 21 d horizon. No hand-tuned perturbation was needed.
  • But unrelated walkers sit at 3.0–4.5 K over the same span. At the horizon siblings are still separated by only about 60% of the unrelated-pair distance, so clones remain correlated at verification and the effective number of independent tail samples is smaller than 64.
  • This is the first quantitative handle on that, and it is the argument for adding the paper’s perturbation if a later run resamples harder or later than this one did.

The stack, in one paragraph

GenCast is the walker: the thing propagated, cloned and killed. FourCastNet 3 is the score function: at each barrier it is handed the walker’s state and asked, with 6 members run to the peak, where that walker is heading. The roles are inverted relative to the obvious reading of the two models — the diffusion model does the physics, the deterministic-core model does the judging. They cannot share a process (JAX versus PyTorch), so walker and scorer hand off through disk, and the join is a 72-channel adapter in which 69 channels are a rename plus a latitude flip and 3 are derived from a fitted calibration.

A separate ten-case follow-up asked whether the adapter costs forecast skill against observed truth, using six extreme events and four quiet-day null controls. The pooled effect is +0.23% with a 95% interval of [−5.20, +5.65] and no regime dependence — so whatever the adapter costs at week 3 lead, it is under about 5% of the native run’s RMSE. For AI+RES that bound is the number that matters: the walker score is an ensemble mean, and a bias this far under the ensemble’s own sampling noise cannot reorder walkers.

OPEN

What this pilot does not settle

  • One event, one atmosphere. Everything here is the 2021 PNW dome from one init. The reach claim is robust; the probability is one draw of one schedule at N = 64.
  • Clones are not independent. 60% of the unrelated-pair separation at verification means the 64 tail samples are worth fewer than 64. Quantified, not yet fixed.
  • The box clips the event. The observed dome maximum reached about 52 N into British Columbia, outside the 50 N CONUS crop the cubes carry, so the scored box sees the southern two thirds of what happened.
  • The far tail overshoots the event. The top-10 composite carries +2.3 K of box bias and the single most extreme walker +2.6 K. Correct for an algorithm told to maximise AL, but it means the extreme end of this population is more extreme than 2021 rather than a reconstruction of it.
  • Absolute probabilities want more walkers. The factor-of-two uncertainty on P is an N problem, and the total-mass diagnostic is the thing to watch as N grows.
